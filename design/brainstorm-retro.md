# Retro inspiration brainstorm (broader, up to about 2000)

Date: 2026-11-25 · Status: **brainstorm. Nothing here is decided; seeds are ideas, not designs.**

## The brief (owner, 2026-11-25)

- The body stays **a small walkie-talkie-like handheld**, because it is something you talk into.
- The core is **push to talk**. Everything else is secondary.
- Inspiration may come from many retro devices, **up to about the year 2000**: cassette players, CD players, game consoles, phones, Walkman, cameras, old Macs, Game Boy and similar. Not limited to the 70s and 80s.
- Borrow the *spirit*, then combine it with the TE-style flat graphic language. The coiled cable is an important signature.

## How to use this note

Evidence level: everything below is my **general design knowledge, not researched in this session**. Please correct me where I am wrong. The rule for borrowing: take the **idea and vocabulary** (a latching key, a thumb wheel, a hold switch). Do **not** copy iconic trade dress: logos, exact silhouettes, famous faces and stripes.

## 2. Ideas for push to talk specifically

1. **Camera-style two-stage press.** Light press = hold to talk; full press with a click = latch; extra firm push = send. (Already our lever. The camera gives users an existing mental model.)
2. **Tape-deck latch.** At the click, the lever *stays down*. A second press, or a stop action, brings it back. The latch becomes visible, not a hidden state.
3. **Walkman HOLD.** A slide switch labelled HOLD that physically blocks the lever and mutes. This is our LOCK, in a language everyone knows.
4. **Game Boy thumb wheel for mode.** A small wheel on the left edge picks DICT / TALK / OPEN; numbers 1-4 around it read as radio channels.
5. **Film-advance arming lever.** A small lever that must be flipped to "arm" the device. A camera-flavoured version of the SAFE / ARMED guard from round 2.
6. **Roger beep.** The "beep" at the end of a walkie-talkie transmission, as an optional sound (already in the fun backlog).

## 3. Form seeds (not drawn yet; body stays walkie-sized)

| **Shutter** | The PTT is a big round **two-stage button on the top shoulder**, pressed by the index finger like a camera; the thumb stays free for the dial | Camera shutter, film-advance lever | Moves the hero off the side; less like the reference |
| **Tape key** | The PTT is a **wide keycap that latches down** and shows its state, like a tape deck | Cassette deck, microcassette recorder | A latching mechanism in FDM |
| **Wheel** | **One control**: a thumb wheel on the left edge: roll to choose the mode, press to talk | Game Boy wheels, mouse wheel | Pressing a wheel for three stages is hard to tune |
| **Face** | A grille and lamp arranged as a tiny abstract face; a carry loop as a handle; optional translucent colours | Old Mac, iMac, Tamagotchi | Must stay abstract; humour only |

The three bodies already drawn in round 4 (Handie, Palm, Pod) come from the **radio** line. These four seeds extend the **retro gadget** line with other ways to do the same job.

## 4. A suggested way forward

- Keep one body language (the Cut family) and **vary the push-to-talk control**, instead of changing the whole body each time.
- Next sketches could take one body (for example Palm) and show **three PTT variants**: side lever (current), shutter button on the shoulder, latching tape key. One body, three controls: the differences are easy to compare.
- Keep the graphic layer: numbers 1-4, small icons, brass screws, an orange coiled cable.

## 5. Questions for the owner

1. Of the devices above, which ones make you feel "yes, that is it"? A name is enough.
2. Do you want the lever to stay a **slanted side bar** (like the reference), or are you open to a **shutter-style** button?
3. Is the idea of a **visible latch** (the key stays down) appealing, or too fussy?
4. Are the iconic references (face, stripe) welcome as an abstract nod, or would you rather avoid them completely?
